Before you choose

Finding your place in Brazil

Every arrival in a new country begins with a search for space: where to live, where to set up the business, where daily life will happen. One premise helps before that choice. No country has a single, homogeneous culture.

China, Italy and Brazil each hold many cultures that share a history and some common traits. Every region, city and neighbourhood combines those traits in its own way.

Stereotypes are a useful starting point. Brazilians are said to be loud, and in many places that holds. Calling a bar or restaurant badalado is praise: it means busy, lively, full of people talking loudly, one of the country’s favourite ways to relax. Brazil also has calm, quiet places made for rest and reflection. They exist in every city; finding them takes knowing where to look.

The state of São Paulo alone has humid, cooler mountain towns and hotter inland cities on the Western São Paulo Plateau, known for its red terra roxa soil and coffee farms. It has metropolises and small towns, and places tied to particular traditions, communities and niches.

In almost any of them you can choose your way of life: quieter or more urban, with more or less security infrastructure, closer to street life or more private, on foot or by car. That choice becomes far easier with someone who lays out the options and reads them with you.

The same reasoning applies to business. A product or service enters Brazil through a whole chain of relationships: the factory, the distributor, the retailer, the consumer, the professionals who recommend, install and repair it, the technical and academic knowledge of the sector, everyday habits, and even the workarounds every culture has.

Knowing whether your product can become part of people’s routines means knowing these places and these people. Part of the answer comes early, through research and listening; part only comes through testing.

The common thread

Across all eight axes, winners did three things: they listened to how Brazilians already used the category, adapted the product or format to that habit, and built trust through the people around the purchase. Those who imported a model unchanged paid for it in years of losses.

01
Cultural axis

Sweetness, affection and memory

In Brazil, sweets carry affection: a grandmother’s kitchen, birthday parties, childhood. The taste has deep roots in the colonial sugar-cane economy and runs through regional cuisines across the country.

What you’ll notice

  • Desserts noticeably sweeter than in Europe or Asia.
  • Brigadeiros at every birthday, for children and adults alike.
  • Coffee often served already sweetened.
  • Homemade sweets offered to visitors as a welcome.

In the room

Accept the sweet offered at the end of a visit or meeting. Asking about a regional dessert is an easy, warm way into conversation.

Success · Foreign brand

Nestlé Leite Moça

Condensed milk reached Brazil in 1890 as the imported “Milkmaid”. Consumers struggled with the English name and called it leite da moça, “the maid’s milk”, after the girl on the label. When Nestlé opened its first Brazilian factory in Araras (São Paulo) in 1921, it adopted the consumers’ nickname as the brand.1,2

Recipes printed on the label moved it from pharmacies to kitchens, and it became the base of the national dessert repertoire.2

The lesson inside the success

The can without recipes

In 2004 Nestlé introduced a curved can without the paper label and its printed recipes. It did not catch on; consumers asked for the recipes back, and the brand restored them.3

Our reading: the recipe on the can carried family memory. Removing it removed part of the product.

For your market entry

A foreign product gains ground when it fits a taste and a memory that already exist. Ask which recipe, occasion or family ritual your product can join, and listen to the names customers give it.

02
Cultural axis

Coffee and hospitality

The cafezinho is a key that opens the door to trust. It is offered to visitors at home, at a company’s reception, in workshops and in offices, often several times a day.

What you’ll notice

  • Coffee brewed in the morning and kept in a thermos flask all day, ready for whoever arrives.
  • Small cups, frequent offers, often pre-sweetened.
  • Brewed, filtered coffee as the everyday standard at home.

In the room

Accept the coffee, and compliment it. Praising the coffee a host offers signals that you value their hospitality, and it often opens conversations a formal agenda would not.

Success · Foreign brand

Melitta

The German company opened a subsidiary in São Paulo in 1968 to sell paper filters and filter holders, in a country where the cloth filter was a household tradition.4 The paper filter kept the familiar gesture of straining coffee and added convenience and hygiene.

1968
arrival in Brazil with paper filters and holders
1980
launches Brazil’s first vacuum-packed coffee
1995–99
adds cappuccino, espresso beans and thermos flasks

Melitta then moved into roasting and packaging, following the ritual from filter to cup to flask.5

Our reading: the innovation kept the gesture Brazilians already loved and improved the experience around it. Innovations that ask people to abandon a ritual travel far more slowly.

For your market entry

Coffee is both a market and a language. Products that respect the ritual of brewed, shared coffee have an open path, and executives who accept and value the coffee they are offered build rapport faster.

03
Cultural axis

Neighbourhood, referral and personal ties

Referrals and personal relationships often weigh as much as the brand. The logic crosses all income levels: from the neighbourhood mechanic to the family lawyer or real-estate broker, Brazilians buy through people they trust.

What you’ll notice

  • Small neighbourhood stores (depósitos) thriving next to big chains, often with informal credit.
  • Homeowners hire a mason or electrician instead of doing it themselves.
  • “Who do you recommend?” as the first step of most purchases.

In the room

Warm introductions open doors that cold outreach does not. Ask for introductions, and expect first meetings to focus on getting to know each other.

Failure · Regional benchmark

Home Depot in South America

The US do-it-yourself giant never operated in Brazil. It entered Chile and Argentina in the late 1990s and in 2001 sold all nine stores: five in Chile to its partner Falabella, four in Argentina to Jumbo, owner of Easy.6,7

Our reading: the model assumed customers would renovate their own homes, in a region where people hire a professional.

Failure · Brazilian innovation

Gurgel

The Brazilian carmaker launched the BR-800 in 1988 with a tax advantage. In 1990 the incentive was extended to every car up to 1.0 litre, and Fiat’s Uno Mille arrived at a similar price with more space and power.8 Gurgel went bankrupt in 1994.9

Our reading: neighbourhood mechanics knew the Uno and its parts; the Gurgel needed rare parts and know-how.

Adaptation

Leroy Merlin

The French retailer kept its DIY message and added Obramax, a cash-and-carry for construction professionals.10

For your market entry

Map who recommends, installs and repairs your product. A product that the neighbourhood professional knows and trusts circulates much faster.

04
Cultural axis

The masonry house in a tropical climate

Brazilian homes are built with brick, concrete and plaster, and this building culture shapes what a home product can be. The workforce is trained in masonry, while drywall and timber systems find few specialists, and the sector also faces a labour shortage.

What you’ll notice

  • No central heating, and hot-water pipes only in higher-end buildings.
  • An electric shower head in almost every bathroom, with wiring close to the water.
  • Water tanks on the roof feeding taps by gravity (see Axis 05).

In the room

On a site visit, talk to the team. Masons and electricians are respected sources of practical knowledge, and their opinion shapes what clients buy.

Local solution

The electric shower

With mild winters across most of the country, there was never demand for central heating. Standard plumbing uses PVC pipes for cold water; hot-water piping (copper, CPVC, PPR) appears mainly in higher-end construction.

The electric shower heats water at the point of use, works with the low pressure of rooftop tanks, costs little and can be installed by any electrician. Decades of relatively cheap hydroelectric power helped make it the standard.

Foreign visitors are often alarmed by the wiring near the water. The device is designed for it, with an insulated heating element and grounding; the real risk comes from improvised installation.

Watch point

Voltage and standards

Mains voltage in Brazil varies by city: 127 V in some, 220 V in others, sometimes both in the same building. Plugs follow a national standard of their own.

Our reading: many imported appliances and building systems fail on details like these before they ever reach the question of price.

For your market entry

Before launching a home product, check how it works with Brazilian construction, electrical and plumbing standards, and with the skills of the professionals who will install it.

05
Cultural axis

Autonomy and material ingenuity

Faced with unreliable public services, Brazilian households build their own guarantees at home. From this need comes an ingenuity with materials: simple, affordable, clever solutions that became national standards.

What you’ll notice

  • Blue water tanks on almost every roof.
  • Gas cylinders delivered to the kitchen by truck.
  • Walls, gates, electric fences, guard booths and doormen around homes and buildings.

In the room

Visible security is normal in offices and residential buildings. Expect ID checks at reception and allow extra minutes before meetings.

Success · Brazilian company

Water tanks and Fortlev

Under Brazilian standards, mains water rises to a household tank and flows down to the taps, with a reserve for at least 24 hours of consumption.11 The reason is intermittent supply.12 Market leader Fortlev was founded in Espírito Santo in 1989.13

06
Cultural axis

Trust through an easy way out

Brazilian consumers take risks when they know they can reach a person and undo the deal. The guarantee of a simple return often weighs as much as the price.

What you’ll notice

  • Customer service through WhatsApp, with real people answering.
  • Buyers checking seller ratings before any online purchase.
  • Strong consumer-protection culture, with Procon offices in every state.

In the room

Availability builds confidence. Quick, personal replies, often by WhatsApp, reassure partners as much as a contract clause does.

Success · Foreign brand

Mercado Livre

Founded in Buenos Aires in 1999, the marketplace entered Brazil when buying from strangers online felt risky. It built trust in layers: protected payment through Mercado Pago, which holds funds until delivery; public seller reputations; and, later, fast logistics with simple returns.

Our reading: each layer lowered the cost of being wrong. Once buyers knew they could undo a purchase, they bought more often.

For your market entry

Reduce the risk your customer perceives. Accessible service, a clear exchange policy and returns without bureaucracy often convert better than discounts.

07
Cultural axis

The shopping routine: deals, instalments, proximity

Brazilians hunt for deals, pay in instalments and spread their shopping across several places: bakery, butcher, street market, neighbourhood grocer. Many make that circuit on foot or by public transport.

What you’ll notice

  • Weekly promotional flyers and “offer days” for specific products.
  • Prices shown as instalments: “10x R$ 49”.
  • Daily trips to the bakery; weekly trips to the street market.

In the room

Payment terms are part of the price conversation. Expect instalments and deadlines to be negotiated alongside the headline number.

Failure · Foreign brand

Walmart Brasil (1995–2018)

Walmart bet on fixed low prices and large stores in a country that shops by promotion, close to home and often without a car, and it never turned its regional chains into a single operation.

Retail analysts point to the imported “Everyday Low Price” policy as a central mistake.18 French rivals Carrefour and Casino adapted to Brazilians’ habit of shopping around for the best deals.20 Early product mixes even included snow boots and golf clubs.21

Our reading: the format asked customers to change their routine. Competitors built around it.

For your market entry

Store format and payment terms matter as much as the product. Visible promotions, instalments and presence along the customer’s daily route make the difference.

08
Cultural axis

Sociability, shared space and status

Brazil has an intense public cultural life (street carnival, June festivals, samba circles) that grows mostly from communities and families. Permanent public leisure infrastructure is scarcer, so social life has moved into malls, condominium clubs, backyards and rooftops.

What you’ll notice

  • Shopping malls working as the city’s safe public square.
  • Condominiums with party rooms, barbecue areas and pools.
  • Music played out loud on beaches, at barbecues and in bars.

In the room

A lively, noisy restaurant signals a good place. An invitation to a barbecue or family dinner is a sign the relationship is moving forward; accept it.

Success · Foreign brand

JBL’s speaker is present at the beach, the barbecue and the rooftop party. Its Brazilian team refused to launch global products without local testing and created products of its own.22

In the room

Business etiquette essentials

In Brazil, business follows relationship. These field practices help you build the bond that makes negotiations flow.

Greeting

A firm handshake, eye contact and a smile. Between acquaintances, men may add a hug or a pat on the shoulder, and women often greet with a kiss on the cheek. Follow the other person’s lead.

Conversation first

Family, food, football and travel open most meetings. Going straight to the agenda can read as cold; a few minutes of conversation build the trust the agenda needs.

Coffee

Accept it and compliment it. The cafezinho is an act of hospitality, and valuing it is valuing your host.

Meals

Business lunches are long and relational. Much of the real alignment happens at the table, after the formal meeting.

Names and titles

First names come quickly. Doutor or Doutora is still used as a mark of respect for lawyers, physicians and senior figures.

Reading “yes” and “no”

Direct refusals are usually softened. “Vamos ver” (“let’s see”) may mean no. Read the tone and follow up personally.

WhatsApp

The main channel for business as well as personal life, including voice messages. A quick reply signals commitment.

Time

Business meetings in São Paulo tend to start on time or close to it; social events run on a more flexible clock. Traffic is a shared, accepted reason for delay; send a message.

Language

Portuguese is the language of business. A few words of it are warmly received; assuming Spanish is spoken is a common misstep.

Continuity

Relationships belong to people as much as to companies. When a contact changes, a personal handover protects the trust built so far.
